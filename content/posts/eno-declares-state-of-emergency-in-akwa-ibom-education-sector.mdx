Governor Umo Eno has taken decisive action on a crisis that has weighed on many of us from home—the deteriorating state of education in Akwa Ibom. By declaring a state of emergency in the sector, he's signaling that what has long felt like a slow-motion catastrophe now demands urgent, coordinated intervention.

The governor's approval to hire 2,000 additional teachers represents something more than a mere bureaucratic exercise. For those of us watching from abroad, this move touches on a deeply personal wound: knowing that younger siblings, cousins, and children back in Akwa Ibom are learning in overcrowded classrooms with understaffed schools. The teacher shortage has been one of the most visible symbols of how fragile our education system has become.

Whether this emergency declaration translates into real change depends on execution. Will these teachers be properly vetted and paid consistently? Will infrastructure keep pace with recruitment? Will there be meaningful oversight? These are the questions we've learned to ask after years of announcements that didn't quite reach their promises.

Still, there's something worth acknowledging here: a governor who recognizes that letting the education sector deteriorate further isn't just a policy failure—it's a generational betrayal. For those of us who carried Akwa Ibom education abroad, who built our lives partly on the foundation it gave us, this emergency declaration feels like someone finally saying out loud what we've all known: this can't continue.

The children coming up in Akwa Ibom deserve better. This move suggests someone is listening, even if the real work is just beginning.
