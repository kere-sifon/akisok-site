There's something stirring in the diaspora community—a real sense that home and abroad don't have to be worlds apart. Recently, Governor Eno travelled to the UK to meet with Akwa Ibom citizens abroad, and the conversations that happened there suggest a promising shift in how we think about development back home.

The focus was on the ARISE Agenda, the governor's blueprint for transforming the state. But what made this particular engagement significant was the recognition that those of us scattered across the Atlantic have something valuable to contribute—not just financially, though remittances matter, but through expertise, networks, and genuine partnership.

The meetings with a UK borough and local diaspora groups centred on structured exchanges: what could knowledge-sharing look like? How might we facilitate projects that benefit our communities? These aren't vague aspirations—they're the beginnings of concrete agreements between state institutions and organised diaspora voices.

For many of us who've left but never really left, this resonates deeply. We've watched helplessly from abroad, hoping someone would finally ask: what do we have to offer beyond the money we send home? What if our professional experience, our understanding of how things work elsewhere, could actually shape policy and development back in Akwa Ibom?

The real test, of course, will be implementation. Agreements signed in UK boardrooms need to translate into tangible outcomes—whether that's skills transfer programmes, business partnerships, or institutional reforms informed by global best practices. But the conversation itself marks progress. It signals that the state sees its diaspora not as an afterthought, but as a stakeholder in its future.
