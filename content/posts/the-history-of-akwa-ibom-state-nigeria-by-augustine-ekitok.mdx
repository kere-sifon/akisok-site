Akwa Ibom wasn't always its own state. For many of us in the diaspora, it might feel like our homeland has existed forever as a distinct entity, but the reality is more recent than we might think. Just over three decades ago—on September 23, 1987—what is now Akwa Ibom was carved out from Cross River State, becoming Nigeria's newest state at the time.

This moment marked a turning point for the region and its people. The creation of Akwa Ibom as an independent state gave the people a political space to chart their own course, develop their own institutions, and assert their distinct identity on the national stage. It was recognition, in many ways, of the unique cultural and linguistic heritage that defines us.

That heritage remains as vibrant today as it was then. Four major languages—Ibibio, Annang, Eket, and Oron—reflect the rich tapestry of our people. These aren't merely dialects; they're repositories of our history, our values, and our ways of knowing the world. And the strong Christian faith that characterizes our state has shaped everything from our social structures to our values and aspirations.

For those of us abroad, understanding this relatively recent birth of our state adds another layer to our connection with home. We're part of a generation that came of age during or after Akwa Ibom's independence, and that history is woven into our own stories. It reminds us that our state, while young as a political entity, carries the weight of centuries of culture and tradition within its borders.
