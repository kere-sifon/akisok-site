When we talk about resistance to British rule in Nigeria, we often hear about male-led movements and famous male leaders. But the history of our people—Igbo and Ibibio—includes a moment of extraordinary collective action that was driven almost entirely by women.

In November 1929, thousands of women across the region rose up in what became known as the Women's War. Called Ogu Umunwanyi in Igbo and Ekong Iban in Ibibio, this uprising wasn't about abstract political ideals. It was rooted in lived experience: women were being taxed by warrant chiefs appointed by the British, their authority undermined, and their economic independence threatened. The colonial administration had fundamentally disrupted the systems that had allowed women to maintain power and agency in pre-colonial society.

What's remarkable is the scale and coordination. Thousands of women—from villages across what is now Akwa Ibom and surrounding areas—mobilized together. They protested, they confronted colonial officials, and they refused to accept the legitimacy of this new order. For a brief moment, they reclaimed political space that colonialism had tried to erase.

The Women's War didn't succeed in reversing colonial rule, but it exposed the fragility of British authority and forced recognition of women's power. It's a story that deserves to be remembered not just as history, but as evidence of a different kind of leadership and resistance that our mothers and grandmothers organized.

For those of us in the diaspora, it's worth asking: what do we inherit from this legacy? How do we carry forward the memory of women who refused to accept domination?
