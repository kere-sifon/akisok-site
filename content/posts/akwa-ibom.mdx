The useful Akwa Ibom is a map, not a slogan. “Land of Promise” travels well in a speech. It does not tell you why your people fish or farm, or why the capital sits inland while the money in the ground and the water sits elsewhere.

Until 1987 this area was folded into Cross River. The state that exists now is bounded by Cross River to the east, the Bight of Biafra to the south, Rivers and Abia to the west, Abia and Ebonyi to the north. Britannica’s entry is dry on purpose: saltwater mangrove along the coast, tropical rain forest and oil palm as you leave the water. That gradient is the state’s actual economy. Yams, rice, cowpeas, maize, cassava for the pot. Palm and cocoa for cash. Poultry and small livestock. Shrimp off the coast, and boats that go farther than the creek. Lignite under the land; oil fields off it.

Uyo is the capital and it is inland. The university there opened in 1983, before there was a state to name it after. The other names that keep mattering are Oron and Opobo toward the water, Ikot Ekpene inland — an old Annang centre that some people still argue should have been the capital, which is why it was not. The Oron–Ikot Ekpene highway and the Cross River are the older ways through. Britannica gives the area as 7,081 square kilometres and the 2006 census as 3.92 million. Every later headcount is a political argument built on top of those figures.

What the encyclopedia files as “mainly Ibibio” is Ibibio, Annang, Oron and the coastal communities that do not disappear because a gazetteer wanted one noun. If you are reading this from abroad, the fact worth keeping is small and dense: a late state, a coastal economy, an inland capital, more than one language. The map is the take. The sobriquet can wait.
