There's a quiet power in the diaspora effect—the ability of those of us scattered across the world to reach back home and transform what we've learned into tangible progress. Governor Eno appears to understand this deeply, recently making a compelling case for why Akwa Ibom's sons and daughters abroad shouldn't just send money during festive seasons.

The pitch is elegant: instead of the traditional one-way flow of remittances, he's proposing something more structural—what he calls "backwards integration." It's less about charity and more about strategic channeling. Think of it as leveraging the expertise we've accumulated far from home: the technical skills, the professional networks, the entrepreneurial lessons learned in competitive markets elsewhere.

For those of us who left Akwa Ibom seeking opportunities, there's often an unspoken guilt mixed with pride. We've climbed certain ladders that weren't available back home, and we feel the weight of that fortune. But beyond the emotional pull, there's practical potential here. A developer in Lagos, an engineer in Canada, a business strategist in London—each brings specific value that could seed real transformation in the state.

This isn't about romanticizing return or expecting people to sacrifice their current lives. Rather, it's recognizing that diaspora engagement works best when it's intentional and structured around what people actually do well, where they are. Educational partnerships, tech transfer initiatives, mentorship programs, business linkages—these don't require relocation; they require deliberate connection.

The question isn't whether diaspora members care—most do. It's whether there are actual channels waiting to receive that care and transform it into something sustainable. Governor Eno seems to be acknowledging that gap.
