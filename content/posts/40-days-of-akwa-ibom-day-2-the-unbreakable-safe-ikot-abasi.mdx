Ikot Abasi holds one of the most sobering chapters in our people's history—a past we must neither forget nor allow to consume us. This coastal settlement became a critical hub during the transatlantic slave trade, a place where thousands of our ancestors passed through before being forcibly shipped across the ocean. The landscape itself bears witness to this tragedy: the Bridge of No Return stands as a haunting reminder of the final crossing point, where captives took their last steps on familiar soil.

What strikes me about places like Ikot Abasi is how they demand we sit with discomfort. For those of us in the diaspora, these sites represent an ancestral wound—a violent separation that shaped our entire existence abroad. We're the descendants of survivors and resistance, yet we often know little about the specific geography of our displacement.

The historical landmarks scattered across Ikot Abasi aren't just tourist attractions; they're conversations frozen in time. They ask us to understand how commerce, greed, and complicity transformed entire communities. They remind us that our people didn't merely suffer passively—many resisted, organized, and endured.

Visiting or learning about Ikot Abasi is an act of reclamation. It's about honoring memory, understanding our roots, and recognizing that the diaspora experience began not with adventure or choice, but with loss and rupture. For Akwa Ibom people scattered across the world, this history is personal—it runs through our blood and explains so much about who we became.
